1 Scope — and what deliberately did not change

This session finalized the CBF sub-weights (including two new sub-scores) and the CF neighbor-selection rules. The hybrid formula itself is untouched:

Unchanged
Hybrid = 0.7 × CBF + 0.3 × (CF / 5) — the 0.7/0.3 split, the /5 normalization, and the Cold-Start behavior (CF None coerced to 0, weight never reallocated) all stay as documented in the glossary.

The philosophy shift
The subject block grew from 0.35 to 0.60 of CBF. This is a deliberate statement: rank primarily on subject relevance to this booking; demographics (course/year/level) are tie-breakers. Everything else was squeezed to pay for it — Course was not raided specifically for the split.

3 The Specific / General split

General Subject = a field that groups related subjects (Science ⊃ Biology, Physics; Programming ⊃ Data Structures, Python). Maps to the existing Subjects.category column — no schema change. Specific Subject = the exact subject code requested.

Why split at all?

The old signal was all-or-nothing: a tutor teaching Python scored identically to one teaching Ethics when the tutee requested Data Structures. The split gives field-relevant tutors partial credit — the graduated middle tier.

Worked example — tutee requests Data Structures (field: Programming)

TutorTeachesSpecific (0.40)General (0.20)Subject credit
AData Structures, OOP11 (superset)0.60
BPython, Calculus01 (via Python)0.20
CEthics only000.00
DWeb Dev (category NULL)00 (no data, no credit)0.00

Key rules, and the whys

  • Binary, not graded — auditable: point at the tutor's subject list and see why.
  • Superset, not mutually exclusive — an exact-match tutor scores 1 on both. The alternative ("General only when Specific = 0") shrinks the exact-vs-field gap from 0.25 to 0.15 and gives the perfect-case tutor less subject credit (0.25) than the old undivided weight (0.35). Superset keeps exact strictly on top.
  • Implement General as max(Specific, category match) — teaching the exact subject counts as being in its field by definition, no category lookup needed. Data holes can then never harm an exact match.
  • Requested subject only — the tutee's preference list no longer feeds subject matching. Previously a tutor matching any listed subject got full exact-match credit; now list-adjacent tutors earn field-level credit at most, through General.

Course ↔ General overlap — examined and accepted

Same-course students share fields, so General partially re-rewards what Course rewards. Verdict: not a blocker — correlated signals are normal in a weighted sum; the two disagree exactly where it matters (the BS Math tutor who teaches Programming; the BSCS tutor who only lists Ethics). Guardrails applied: Course was lowered (0.20 → 0.10), and the honest defense line is that the latent "same academic field" signal effectively earns Wgeneral plus a chunk of Wcourse.

4 The dominance property (the one-line defense answer)

Guarantee
No field-only tutor can ever outrank an exact-match tutor. Exact-match minimum: 0.40 + 0.20 + minimum expertise (1/5 × 0.15 = 0.03) = 0.63. Field-only theoretical maximum: 0.20 + 0.15 + 0.10 + 0.10 + 0.05 = 0.60, and only with a perfect score on every other signal. This holds because of the superset rule and survives any category data hole.

5 Expertise cascade (option C)

"Use the most specific evidence available" — the same ladder as Specific/General:

1 — ExactTutor teaches the requested subjectThat subject's expertise_level / 5 — their unrelated subjects are ignored

Why not the alternatives: (a) requested-subject-only makes Expertise a redundant shadow of Specific and leaves field-match tutors undifferentiated — a Python master and a Python novice would tie; (b) field-average-for-everyone dilutes an exact-match tutor's known level with unrelated courses (Data Structures 5 + OOP 2 reads as 3.5 when you know they're a 5 at the thing being booked).

7 CF — same-course peer ratings

Goal: predictions feel like "peer ratings" — students on the same curriculum rating the same tutors.

  • Peer Pool: Top-K neighbors drawn from students with exact course equality. No strand tier — strand-mates don't share a curriculum, and a tiered pool (course → strand → global) was rejected as three-pools-to-explain complexity for marginal gain.
  • Null course: empty Peer Pool → the fallback quietly scores from the global pool. The fallback is the null handling; no special case.
  • Positive similarity only (> 0), both pools: small peer pools would otherwise let a negative-similarity "anti-peer" dominate a prediction (their contribution is inverted) and — worse — count as usable peer signal that blocks the global fallback. Zero similarity carries no information and doesn't vote either. This resolves the commented-out if similarity >= 0 already sitting in CF.py.

8 Per-tutor vs per-request fallback

Both neighbor lists (peer + global) are computed once per request. The decision was when to fall back:

Per-request (one pool for the whole ranking)Per-tutor (chosen)
ProsStatistically clean — one population per ranking; simplest story; one top_k callNo tutor zeroed for lack of peer opinion; avoids the new-tutor feedback loop (unrated by peers → ranked low → never booked by peers → never rated); never worse than the current system
ConsTutors your coursemates haven't rated get CF = 0 despite usable global data — a hidden 30% penalty; buries new and cross-course tutors; failure is invisibleMixes populations within one ranking (apples-to-oranges objection); two top_k calls; slightly longer explanation
Fails worst whenSmall programs / new tutors — the platform's current phaseDense rating data — a future state, not the present
Decision
Per-tutor fallback: each prediction uses same-course peers when at least one qualifying peer rated that tutor; otherwise that tutor's prediction falls back to the global pool. Peers always win when they have an opinion, so the "peer ratings" promise holds everywhere it can hold. Rationale: at current data size, missing data is the dominant error, not population mixing — a slightly-noisy real signal beats a clean zero.

9 Scalability plan

Recorded revisit trigger — not a TODO
When rating density is high enough that same-course pools reliably cover most candidate tutors, reconsider per-request fallback for population purity (every CF score in one ranking drawn from a single population). The per-tutor choice is the right starting posture, not a permanent commitment. Until then, the bounded costs are: two top_k calls per request (negligible) and mixed populations within a ranking (accepted).

11 Defense Q&A

On the weights themselves

Q: How did you determine these weights — empirically or by judgment?
A: Design judgment, constrained by provable properties rather than guesses: the weights sum to 1.0; the subject block (0.60) encodes the deliberate policy that subject relevance to the current booking dominates demographics; and the Specific/General ratio was chosen so the exact-match dominance property holds mathematically (0.63 minimum vs 0.60 maximum — section 4). We validate behavior through the superadmin algorithm demo tool, which shows the full per-tutor breakdown. At our data volume, offline learning-to-rank would overfit; principled constraints plus inspectable breakdowns are the honest approach.
Q: Why did subject matching grow from 0.35 to 0.60 of CBF?
A: The recommender ranks tutors for a specific requested subject, so subject relevance should dominate. Under the old weights, a same-course, same-year tutor who didn't teach the subject could score 0.45 while an exact-subject tutor from another course scored 0.55 — too close for signals of such different importance. The new weights make demographics tie-breakers among subject-relevant tutors, which matches what a student actually wants.
Q: Why are Specific and General binary instead of a graded similarity?
A: Auditability. A binary signal can be verified by pointing at the tutor's subject list. Graded similarity (e.g. text embeddings) would need training data we don't have and would make every ranking harder to explain. The graduated behavior we wanted lives in the structure — exact (0.60) > field (0.20) > none (0) — not in fractional sub-scores.
Q: Doesn't General Subject double-count what Course already measures?
A: They correlate but are not identical, and they disagree exactly where it matters: a BS Math tutor teaching Programming subjects (General = 1, Course = 0) or a BSCS tutor listing only Ethics (General = 0, Course = 1). We accounted for the correlation by lowering Course to 0.10 rather than raising it, and we acknowledge that the latent "same academic field" signal effectively earns Wgeneral plus part of Wcourse.
Q: Teaching Level is only 0.05 — why keep it at all?
A: It is a penalty flag, not a ranking signal: it scores 1 for nearly everyone and 0 only for an SHS-level tutor matched to a college student. Its job is to nudge that mismatch down, not to reorder healthy matches, so a small weight is the honest size for it.

On robustness and edge cases

Q: What happens when subject categories are missing or misspelled?
A: Bounded degradation, never breakage. General is implemented as max(Specific, category match), so a data hole can only ever cost fallback credit (at most 0.20 + 0.15) and can never harm an exact-subject match. The system stays correct while two follow-up tickets fix the root cause: admin editing of tutor-proposed subjects and a category hygiene pass.
Q: Can a tutor who merely teaches something in the same field ever beat a tutor who teaches the exact subject?
A: No — provably. The exact-match tutor's floor is 0.63 (Specific 0.40 + General 0.20 + minimum expertise 0.03); the field-only tutor's ceiling is 0.60, and only with perfect course, year, and level. This is a direct consequence of the superset rule and holds under any data-quality failure.
Q: How does the system treat a brand-new student (cold start)?
A: Unchanged by this rebalance: with no rating history CF returns None, coerced to 0, so a Cold-Start Tutee's hybrid score is 0.7 × CBF. The CF weight is deliberately not reallocated — the cap is a documented property of the formula, surfaced in the UI as a Cold Start badge, not a hidden bug.

On the CF changes

Q: Why restrict CF neighbors to the same course? Doesn't that throw away data?
A: It prioritizes, it doesn't discard. Same-course students rate the same tutors on the same curriculum, so their ratings are the most comparable evidence — genuine "peer ratings." When peers have no opinion on a tutor, that tutor's prediction falls back to the global pool, so no data is ever wasted; it is only out-prioritized when better-matched evidence exists.
Q: Isn't mixing peer-based and global-based scores in one ranking statistically impure?
A: Yes, and we accepted that trade-off consciously. At our data density, missing data is the dominant error source, not population mixing — a per-request pool would hand CF = 0 to every tutor our coursemates haven't rated, systematically burying new tutors and creating a never-rated-by-peers feedback loop. We recorded a revisit trigger: once rating density lets peer pools cover most tutors, we reconsider per-request fallback for purity.
Q: Pearson similarity supports negative correlations — why exclude negative-similarity neighbors?
A: Negative weights are only informative with dense data. In a small same-course pool, one opposite-taste neighbor can dominate a prediction with an inverted contribution, and — worse — count as "usable peer signal" that blocks the global fallback. Requiring similarity > 0 means we only learn from demonstrated agreement; zero similarity carries no information, so it doesn't vote either.
Q: Why keep the hybrid at 0.7 CBF / 0.3 CF?
A: CBF-dominant weighting is the standard posture for sparse-rating platforms: profile signals exist for every tutor from day one, while CF quality grows with rating volume. Rebalancing the hybrid was explicitly scoped out of this session; the 0.7/0.3 split is a stable, already-documented decision, and changing two layers at once would make the effect of either change unmeasurable.
Q: How will you verify the new formula actually behaves as designed?
A: Three layers: unit tests asserting the invariants (dominance property, null-category handling, expertise cascade tiers, both fallback levels, the positive-similarity filter); the superadmin algorithm demo tool showing the full sub-score breakdown per tutor and which CF pool each prediction used; and the standard repo checks (backend test suite) before anything merges.
